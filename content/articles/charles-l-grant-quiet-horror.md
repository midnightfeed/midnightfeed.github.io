There is a particular kind of collector who finds Charles L. Grant late and then cannot stop. It usually happens the same way: a battered paperback of something set in Oxrun Station turns up in a fifty-cent bin, gets read on a slow evening, and turns out to be doing something almost nothing else on the horror shelf of its decade was doing. Nothing explodes. Nobody is eaten. The dread arrives sideways, through fog and streetlight and a sentence that does not quite land where you expected it to.

Grant called it quiet horror, and he meant it as an argument. Through the late seventies and the whole of the eighties — the exact years when the mass market was rewarding every possible variety of loud — he kept insisting that suggestion outperforms demonstration, and he kept proving it in a Connecticut town he invented and then haunted across eight novels and a run of linked novellas.

## The anthologist mattered more than the novelist

If Grant had written nothing at all he would still belong in any serious account of the period, because he edited *Shadows*. Eleven volumes for Doubleday between 1978 and 1991, and for most of that run it was simply the best original horror anthology series in English — the place where the quiet story had a guaranteed home when every other market wanted a body count.

{{pullquote}}

He worked under other names as well, which is where the hunt gets interesting. Geoffrey Marsh for the Lincoln Blackthorne adventures, Lionel Fenn for comedy, Simon Lake for the young-adult horror line. Those pseudonymous runs are easy to miss in a listing that never mentions Grant, which is precisely the gap a collector who reads this kind of article learns to work.

## Where to start

Start with *The Hour of the Oxrun Dead*, because it is the front door to everything else, and because it still turns up in a grade you would not be embarrassed to shelve. Then take whichever *Shadows* volume you come across first; they do not need to be read in order, and the contributor lists are an education in who was actually good in 1982. The novels outside the Oxrun cycle are more uneven, but *The Pet* is the one people keep coming back to.

Grant died in 2006. For most of the twenty years since, his paperbacks have been some of the best-value horror on the used shelf. I would not count on that lasting.
